The Last Keeper

Every evening for thirty years, Thomas climbed the hundred and twelve steps of the lighthouse and lit the lamp. He knew every crack in the walls and every sound the wind made as it swept across the rocks. When the letter arrived telling him that the light would soon be automatic, he read it twice, folded it carefully and put it in his pocket without a word.

His daughter, Ellen, came to visit the following weekend. She found him sitting at the kitchen table, staring at the grey sea. "You knew this would happen one day, Dad," she said gently. He nodded, but he did not look at her. "Knowing something and being ready for it are two different things," he replied.

That night a storm came in from the west. The rain hammered against the windows, and the waves crashed so hard against the rocks that the whole tower seemed to tremble. Around midnight, Thomas noticed a faint light out at sea, appearing and disappearing between the waves. A small fishing boat was drifting towards the reef.

He grabbed the radio and called the coastguard, but the signal was weak and the voice at the other end kept breaking up. So he did what he had done many times before: he turned the great lamp slowly towards the boat and held it there, guiding it away from the rocks and into the calmer water of the bay. It took almost an hour. When the boat was finally safe, his hands were shaking.

In the morning, the fishermen knocked on his door to thank him. They were exhausted and soaked to the skin, but they were alive. After they had left, Ellen put her arm around her father's shoulders. "A machine can turn on a light," she said, "but it can't keep watch." For the first time in weeks, Thomas smiled.
