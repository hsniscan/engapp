Cities Are Learning to Live With Extreme Heat

Last summer, temperatures in several European cities rose above 40 degrees for days at a time. Hospitals were busy, trains were delayed, and many older people stayed indoors because the streets felt like ovens. Experts say these heatwaves are no longer rare events. They are becoming a normal part of life, and cities have to adapt.

Some solutions are surprisingly simple. In Paris, the city council has turned dozens of school playgrounds into small green parks. The concrete was removed, trees were planted, and the soil now absorbs rainwater instead of letting it run into the drains. On hot afternoons, these "oasis yards" are several degrees cooler than the surrounding streets, and local residents are allowed to use them when classes are over.

Other cities rely on technology. Engineers in Seville are testing an ancient method of cooling: water flows through underground channels and cools the air above it. The project is still at an early stage, but the first results are encouraging. "We are not inventing anything new," one of the designers admitted. "We are simply rediscovering what people knew a thousand years ago."

Not everyone is convinced. Critics argue that small projects like these cannot compensate for decades of poor planning. Wide roads, dark roofs and a lack of shade have turned many neighbourhoods into heat traps. Changing that will require huge investment, and city budgets are already under pressure. Moreover, the poorest districts, which suffer the most, often receive the least attention.

Still, there are reasons for optimism. Painting roofs white, for instance, is cheap and can reduce the temperature inside a building considerably. Planting trees takes longer, but the benefits last for generations. As one urban planner put it, the question is no longer whether cities should prepare for extreme heat, but how quickly they can do it.
