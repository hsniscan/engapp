Why We Need Sleep

For a long time, scientists could not explain why we spend about a third of our lives asleep. From an evolutionary point of view, sleep seems risky: a sleeping animal cannot look for food, and it is easy prey for predators. Yet almost every animal that has been studied sleeps in some form, which suggests that sleep must be essential.

Recent research has provided some answers. One of the most important functions of sleep appears to be cleaning the brain. During the day, waste products build up between the nerve cells. While we sleep, the space between the cells expands, and fluid flushes these substances away. Some researchers believe that when this process does not work properly, the risk of diseases such as Alzheimer's may increase.

Sleep also plays a crucial role in memory. In a typical experiment, volunteers learn a list of words in the evening. Half of them are allowed to sleep normally, while the other half are kept awake. The next morning, the people who slept remember significantly more words. It seems that during sleep the brain replays what happened during the day and stores the most useful information.

Lack of sleep has obvious short-term effects. People who sleep badly find it harder to concentrate, make more mistakes and are more likely to lose their temper. Over longer periods, poor sleep has been linked to weight gain, high blood pressure and a weaker immune system.

So how much sleep do we actually need? Most adults function best with seven to nine hours a night, although the exact amount varies from person to person. Teenagers need more, and their natural rhythm makes them tend to fall asleep and wake up later. Experts therefore recommend keeping a regular schedule, avoiding screens before bedtime and making sure the bedroom is dark and quiet. These habits may sound simple, but for many people they make a remarkable difference.
